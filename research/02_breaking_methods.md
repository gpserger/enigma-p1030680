# Enigma Cryptanalysis: A Survey for Attacking a 72-Letter Naval Intercept

**Purpose.** This is a working reference for implementing a code-based attack on a genuine, unsolved 72-letter WWII German naval Enigma ciphertext (see `/opt/enigma-p1030680/ciphertext.txt`, confirmed 72 letters, no visible discriminant group separated out). It covers what Bletchley Park actually did, what modern ciphertext-only cryptanalysis can do, and — the load-bearing part — hard numbers on how short a message can be broken today, with a real 72-letter break as a calibration point.

**A note on sourcing discipline.** Numbers below are either (a) quoted/paraphrased directly from a primary source, in which case the source is cited inline, or (b) derived by me from those sources by straightforward arithmetic, in which case they are explicitly marked **[DERIVED]** and the calculation is shown. Do not treat derived numbers as literature values — verify them empirically against your own simulator before relying on them for planning. Anything I could not find a source for is marked **[NOT ESTABLISHED IN SOURCES FOUND]** rather than guessed.

## 0. Executive orientation

- Three-rotor, steckered Wehrmacht **Army** Enigma ciphertexts as short as **72 letters have already been broken ciphertext-only**, with a fully documented, reproducible example (message *CFYZR*, 14 July 1941, Heeresgruppe Nord) — see §2.4 and the verified test vector in §2.5. This is your single best sanity-check target: build your attack, confirm it reproduces this known break, *then* point it at the real target.
- Your target is (presumably) **naval** Enigma, which has a larger keyspace than the Army case above (336 vs 60 wheel orders for M3; possibly a 4th rotor for M4) and a different, historically much harder to break, indicator system. Section 4 quantifies the gap; §6 gives the odds.
- A real **ciphertext-only break of a 128-letter genuine naval M4 message** exists (Hörenberg's U-534 project, §3.3), which is closer in spirit to your problem than any Army example, but not as short as 72 letters.
- If any crib is available — even a probable word or a known message-format fragment — the problem becomes vastly easier (§3), because plugboard hillclimbing from a crib-anchored partial key is a different, much better-conditioned problem than pure ciphertext-only search.

## 1. Historical cryptanalysis (Bletchley Park, 1939–1945)

### 1.1 Cribs and the Bombe: menus and loops

Bletchley Park's Hut 6/Hut 8 attack on Army/Luftwaffe and (differently) naval Enigma was fundamentally **known-plaintext**: a *crib* (a guessed plaintext fragment, e.g. a stereotyped phrase or a word believed to occur at a known or unknown position) was aligned against the ciphertext. Because Enigma never enciphers a letter to itself, a crib alignment could be falsified immediately wherever crib letter = ciphertext letter — this alone eliminates most alignments.

From a valid crib/ciphertext alignment, the cryptanalyst built a **menu**: a graph where letters are nodes and each crib-position pairing (plaintext letter ↔ ciphertext letter, at a given machine offset) is an edge. Chains of edges that closed back on themselves formed **loops** (Turing's "closures"). Loops were valuable because they let the Bombe test a hypothesized plugboard letter and immediately derive logical consequences around the loop without needing to know any other plugboard settings — this is what made the Bombe's electromechanical contradiction-search (looking for a rotor position where the tested hypothesis does *not* lead to a contradiction) tractable. Menus with more closures needed shorter cribs for a given false-stop rate: Bletchley Park's standard was to use only menus estimated to produce ≤4 stops per wheel order, which meant **an 8-letter crib sufficed for a 3-closure menu, 11 letters for a 2-closure menu, and 14 letters for a menu with only one closure** (Wikipedia, "Cryptanalysis of the Enigma").

The Bombe itself mechanically ran the wheel-order × position search implied by the menu and flagged positions with no logical contradiction (Turing–Welchman Bombe; see Davies 1999, cited in Ostwald & Weierud 2017).

### 1.2 Banburismus: exploiting the naval indicator system statistically

Banburismus, developed by Turing, was Hut 8's technique for narrowing the naval Enigma **wheel order** search before committing scarce Bombe time. It exploited **depths**: because all naval messages on a given day were enciphered from the same basic setting (*Grundstellung*) in the indicator procedure of that period, pairs of message indicators sometimes aligned "in depth" — i.e., overlapping rotor positions across two different messages. Operators punched ciphertexts onto long strips ("Banbury sheets", named for the town where they were printed) and slid them against each other over a light box, looking for above-chance letter coincidences between the two texts at a given offset — German (and specifically naval) plaintext repeats a letter at the same offset roughly **1 time in 17 (≈5.9%)**, versus **1 in 26 (≈3.8%)** for random text.

Turing quantified the evidential weight of each such coincidence using a log-odds unit he called the **ban** (and the finer-grained **deciban**, one tenth of a ban), an early, explicit application of sequential Bayesian updating to a real cryptanalytic decision problem: successive independent pieces of evidence (matches/non-matches at various offsets, across many message pairs) were summed as decibans until the accumulated score crossed a threshold for "these two messages are (or are not) in depth at this offset," and from a large set of depths, which two of the three wheels were in the **right-hand and middle positions** could be inferred (the third, left-hand wheel identity, and the stecker, still had to come from the Bombe run). This pruned the full **336** naval wheel-order space down to roughly **18** wheel orders worth running on the Bombe (Banburismus Wikipedia article; corroborated by the search summary above).

### 1.3 Herivel tip and cillies

Two operator-error-driven shortcuts mattered enormously, especially for Army/Luftwaffe traffic and especially in specific periods of the war:

- **Herivel tip (Herivelismus).** John Herivel (arrived Bletchley Park January 1940) reasoned about operator laziness: after setting the rotor *rings* to the day's prescribed ring setting and closing the machine lid, a rushed operator would often leave the *rotor windows* (start position) very close to the ring setting rather than spinning them far away, because doing so was the path of least resistance. Plotting the observed initial rotor-window letters from many intercepted messages on a given day would then cluster near the true ring setting, letting Bletchley guess the day's ring setting directly from operator sloppiness. This, combined with cillies, was the dominant Army attack method for a critical stretch after May 1940.
- **Cillies.** A broad category of operator errors: e.g. keying obvious, guessable message-key choices such as keyboard-adjacent letter sequences, repeated single letters (AAA, BBB), or operators' own initials/girlfriends' names as the "random" start position, and re-use of the previous message's final rotor position as the next message's start position. These gave Bletchley Park either a direct guess at the key or a strong statistical bias to search first.

Both were operator-procedure weaknesses, not mathematical weaknesses of Enigma itself, and both were specific to services/periods with looser discipline — the Kriegsmarine's indicator procedure (below) structurally prevented most of this class of shortcut.

### 1.4 Kisses / JN and inter-network cribs

Bletchley Park routinely exploited **re-transmission of the same message content on two different networks with different (and differently broken) encipherment** — nicknamed "kisses." If the same signal went out once on a lower-grade or already-broken system and again on Enigma, the plaintext (or a very close paraphrase) of the Enigma version was known, providing a crib without any guesswork. This was one of the most reliable crib sources precisely because it required no assumption about German phrasing at all.

### 1.5 Captured material: why naval Enigma required "pinches"

The Kriegsmarine's indicator system, introduced 1 May 1937, replaced the Army/Luftwaffe-style doubled-indicator scheme (which leaked information through indicator repetition) with the **Kenngruppenbuch (K-book)** system:

1. The operator picked two random trigrams from the Kenngruppenbuch: a *Schlüsselkenngruppe* (key/net identification group) and a *Verfahrenkenngruppe* (process group used to derive the actual message key).
2. The two trigrams (6 letters) were arranged into a 2×3 grid with one filler letter, then each column was substituted **vertically** through a **bigram substitution table**, the *Doppelbuchstabentauschtafel* — e.g., an example transformation XSWQ/RAFP → VGVX/IFTT, transmitted as the 8-letter group "VIGF VTXT" preceding the enciphered message (Wikipedia, "Discriminant Book").
3. A set of **nine different bigram tables** existed; a calendar/schedule determined which table applied on which day (Wikipedia, "Discriminant Book" via cryptomuseum.com).

Crucially, **without knowing the correct bigram table, an intercepted naval message's indicator is just noise** — there is no repeated-letter or doubled-indicator structure to exploit the way Hut 6 exploited Army/Luftwaffe traffic. This is precisely *why* naval Enigma was so much harder, and why **captured physical material** became indispensable rather than merely helpful:

- **U-110** (captured 9 May 1941): documents reaching Bletchley Park in June 1941 gave Hut 8 the bigram tables needed to even *start* attacking Dolphin (general naval) traffic — "without the bigram tables, Hut 8 were unable to start attacking the traffic" (search summary, corroborated across sources).
- **U-559** (boarded while sinking, Mediterranean, 30 October 1942): Lieutenant Anthony Fasson, Able Seaman Colin Grazier and canteen assistant Tommy Brown recovered a copy of the **Wetterkurzschlüssel** (short weather cipher book) and the **Kurzsignalheft** (short signal book); these reached Bletchley Park 24 November 1942. This directly broke the ten-month "Shark blackout" (see §1.6).

### 1.6 The four-rotor M4 and the "Shark" blackout

The Kriegsmarine introduced a **fourth, non-rotating rotor** (Walze Beta or Gamma, paired with a correspondingly thinner reflector, UKW B-thin or C-thin) for U-boat traffic (network "Triton"/"Shark") starting **1 February 1942**. Bletchley Park's three-rotor Bombes could not attack this directly, and Hut 8 went effectively **dark on U-boat traffic for about 10 months**, a period historically called the "Shark blackout," during which U-boat wolfpacks operated with much greater impunity (43 ships sunk in August–September 1942 alone, per search summary). The break came via the U-559 pinch: the **Wetterkurzschlüssel**, used to send abbreviated weather reports, let the fourth rotor be effectively "wound back" to a neutral position (the weather short-signal system used the M4 in a mode compatible with three-rotor M3 weather-ship traffic, fourth rotor at position A / ring A), making those specific messages crackable on existing three-rotor Bombes and from there recovering the daily key. Shark was first broken again **13 December 1942**, and subsequently Hut 8 broke it on 90 of 112 days in the run-up to 30 June 1943 (search summary of Wikipedia "Hut 8" / "Short Weather Cipher").

**Takeaway for a modern attacker:** the entire historical route into naval Enigma ran through captured physical material and message-format regularities (the weather short-signal system), *not* through a mathematical weakness beyond what Army Enigma had. A modern ciphertext-only attacker has no equivalent of the bigram tables or the Kenngruppenbuch and must substitute raw computation plus language-statistics scoring for what Bletchley substituted with pinches.

## 2. Modern ciphertext-only cryptanalysis

### 2.1 Core building blocks

- **Index of Coincidence (IC).** Friedman's 1920 statistic; for a monoalphabetic-like candidate text, IC is close to the plaintext language's natural value (~6.1–7.6% depending on corpus, per Ostwald & Weierud below) when the candidate is close to correct, and close to random (~3.8%) otherwise. IC is cheap to compute and, critically, is largely insensitive to plugboard errors early in a search because an empty or partially-correct plugboard still leaves the text "more monoalphabetic than random" — this is why it works as the *first-stage* discriminator even before any stecker is known.
- **N-gram scoring (bigram/trigram/quadgram/hexagram).** Log-probability scores against a reference corpus of the target language (here, WWII German military traffic — *not* ordinary German; see §2.4's warning about specialized vocabulary). Generally more discriminating than IC once the candidate is closer to correct, but more sensitive to garbles (a single wrong letter corrupts several overlapping n-grams) and to plugboard errors (each wrong stecker corrupts many n-grams at once).
- **Hillclimbing / simulated annealing on the plugboard.** Because the plugboard is a fixed involution applied identically to every letter throughout the message (unlike the rotors, which change state every keystroke), once a candidate (wheel order, ring settings, start position) is fixed, finding the best plugboard is a **local search over a monoalphabetic-substitution-like space**: try adding/swapping a plug, keep the change if the scoring function improves, and repeat. This is the same idea used to break simple substitution ciphers, adapted because Enigma's plugboard is an *involution* (self-inverse, and it also acts on the *ciphertext* on the way in, not just the plaintext on the way out) rather than an arbitrary substitution.

### 2.2 Gillogly (1995) — the founding ciphertext-only method

James Gillogly's 1995 Cryptologia paper ("Ciphertext-only Cryptanalysis of Enigma") established the modern two-stage template:

1. **Stage 1 (wheel order + position, IC-scored):** exhaustively try all 60 wheel orders × 26³ (17,576) starting positions, **with the plugboard left empty and ring settings held constant**, and score each candidate decryption by IC. Because IC survives an empty plugboard reasonably well on long texts, the correct wheel order and start position "shine through" even before the stecker is known. This reduced the space to exactly **60 × 26³ = 1,054,560 cases — the same number of cases the wartime Bombes handled** (Ostwald & Weierud 2017, p.3, directly comparing Gillogly's exhaustion count to the Bombe's).
2. **Stage 2 (ring settings):** using the best wheel-order/position candidates from stage 1, exhaust the 676 middle/right ring-setting combinations.
3. **Stage 3 (plugboard via hillclimbing):** switch the scoring function from IC to a trigram count (more efficient once close to correct) and hillclimb the plugboard: try a trial plug, keep it if the trigram score improves the candidate text, discard otherwise.

**Concrete numbers from the paper (as reported in Ostwald & Weierud 2017, p.3):** Gillogly's technique worked well on **messages around 300 letters with up to six plugs**, and on **longer messages with up to ten plugs**; his only success with all ten plugs used was a **1463-letter message**. His demonstration case used a **647-letter** message.

### 2.3 Williams (2000) and Sullivan & Weierud (2005)

- **Heidi Williams (2000)**, "Applying Statistical Language Recognition Techniques in the Ciphertext-Only Cryptanalysis of Enigma" (Cryptologia 24(1):4–17), investigated alternative candidate-quality statistics and found the **Sinkov statistic** most useful, improving on Gillogly's approach; she demonstrated successful decryption of a **non-authentic, synthetic 500-letter English message enciphered with all ten plugs used** (Ostwald & Weierud 2017, p.13, note this is beyond the German 250-letter wartime regulation length and is a synthetic, not genuine, text).
- **Geoff Sullivan & Frode Weierud (2005)**, "Breaking German Army Ciphers" (Cryptologia 29(3):193–232) — the first modern ciphertext-only attack on **genuine, authentic WWII Enigma ciphertext**: ~several hundred surviving Army (Heeresgruppe Nord, "HG Nord") messages from Operation Barbarossa, 1941, three-rotor steckered Wehrmacht Enigma (**60 wheel orders, not naval's 336** — HG Nord is Army/Wehrmacht traffic, not Kriegsmarine). They devised an improved **three-pass hillclimbing** technique: use IC to find the first ~4 plugs, switch to **bigram scoring** for the next phase, then **trigram scoring** for the final phase — and found that plain German text is a poor training corpus, because Wehrmacht traffic used specialized abbreviations and conventions (e.g. representing the German digraphs "ch"/"ck" with a single letter "Q"). Their **first break of a genuine HG Nord message was achieved 17 March 2003**: message No. 25 (5-letter discriminant *FHPQX*), 214 letters, translating to "To Tank Group 4: SST Div stands since 12 July 1100 hours..." — cited and reproduced in Ostwald & Weierud 2017 with the full key and ciphertext (see §2.5).

### 2.4 Ostwald & Weierud (2017) — pushing into short-message territory

Olaf Ostwald & Frode Weierud, "Modern Breaking of Enigma Ciphertexts" (*Cryptologia* 41(5):395–421, version 5 PDF at cryptocellar.org, read directly for this survey), is the single most load-bearing modern paper for a 72-letter target, and its methods and numbers below are quoted/paraphrased directly from the primary text.

**Corpus and problem framing.** They worked from ~500 authentic HG Nord messages (Army, three-rotor, 60 wheel orders, 10 plugs — again, **not** naval), lengths ranging 15–322 letters (mean 126, median 103), and specifically targeted the subset under 80 letters, down to ~30 letters, that resisted the Sullivan/Weierud 2005 method.

**Unicity distance for Enigma [primary, from the paper].** Using Shannon's H/D formula, with H≈72 bits (log₂ of the keyspace, 150,738,274,937,250 stecker arrangements × 26,364,000 wheel-order/position/ring combinations, assuming no left-wheel turnover) and D≈3.1 bits/character plaintext redundancy for HG Nord German military text, they compute a **unicity distance of ≈23 letters**. Their practical conclusion: **messages shorter than ~20 letters are virtually unbreakable in principle** (not enough information exists in the ciphertext to distinguish the true key from spurious candidates, even with unlimited compute), and **the empirical turning point where some short messages become breakable is around 24 letters**, though this is not a sharp threshold — breakability depends heavily on the individual message's IC and garble rate, not just length. As a striking illustration, they show that both very short authentic HG Nord messages (18, 22, 24 letters) and short-but-longer ones (46, 69, 76 letters) can have similar, low, near-random ICs purely by chance, while some 40-80-letter messages "broke surprisingly easily" and others "proved nearly unbreakable" purely as a function of their individual accidental statistics, not their length per se.

**Why the standard scoring pipeline breaks down under ~80 letters.** IC becomes unreliable as the primary discriminator for finding the *first few* correct plugs once message length drops below ~80 letters (its natural statistical variance at short lengths, σ≈0.4% for a 69-letter text, means the difference between a "close to right" candidate and a random one can be within noise). Bigram scoring is *also* unreliable for short/garbled texts, because accidental high-value bigrams (EN, ER, RE) occur "regularly rather than rarely" in short garbled candidate decryptions and actively mislead the hillclimb down a wrong path. **Their conclusion: for short messages there is no reliable criterion for finding the first few correct plugs at all — the only robust answer is targeted exhaustion, not statistical discrimination.** Trigram scoring remains useful for finding the *last* few plugs (both short and long messages, garbled or not); hexagram (6-gram) scoring is excellent for long, garble-free text but loses effectiveness on garbled short text (HG Nord messages commonly carried 5–30%+ garbles from radio transmission/transcription).

**Partial plugboard exhaustion — the key technique for short messages.** Rather than starting hillclimbing from an empty plugboard (which works for long messages, since the six inherently-correct self-steckered letters give the hillclimb a foothold) or fully exhausting all 150+ trillion plugboard arrangements (infeasible), they **exhaustively try all possible values of the first one, two, three, or four plugs**, then hillclimb the rest:
- **"Solo"** (exhaust 1 plug fully): 325 cases (25+24+...+1) — combined with the 27,418,560 "locations" (wheel order × position × right-hand ring, see §4), gives 8,911,032,000 total cases.
- **"Duet"** (exhaust 2 plugs in parallel): 44,850 cases per location.
- **"Trio"** (3 plugs): 3,453,450 cases per location (becomes impractically slow).
- **"Quartet"** (4 plugs): 164,038,875 cases per location (too slow on their 2017-era hardware).
- **Frequency-weighted variants**, their most practical recommendation: instead of exhausting *all* letters for the first plug, only exhaust plugs involving the **most frequent plaintext letters** (from a monogram histogram over ~500 decrypted HG Nord messages: E, N, X, R are most frequent; C, P, J, Y are rarest). **"E-Stecker"** exhausts all 26 possible partners for E alone (>10× faster than "solo," which tries every letter); **"R-Stecker"** exhausts E, N, and R together (73 cases); **"I-Stecker"** exhausts E, N, R, X, S, and I together (136 cases). **I-Stecker is reported as "a very efficient technique, which solves many short messages."**

**Left-wheel turnover and the "ignore middle-wheel ring" shortcut.** Because most short messages don't run long enough for the left-hand wheel to step at all, Ostwald & Weierud argue the **middle-wheel ring setting can be ignored** (fixed arbitrarily) for short messages, leaving only the **right-hand wheel's 26 ring settings** to search — collapsing the "ring settings" dimension from 676 to 26 and giving their combined **"locations" count of 60 × 26⁴ = 27,418,560** for the Army case. **Caveat for naval wheels (my own derivation, not in the paper — see §4.3): this shortcut is much less safe for naval Enigma**, because three of the eight naval rotors (VI, VII, VIII) have **two notches** instead of one, making a left-wheel turnover roughly **2–4× more likely** within a 72-letter message than for the Army's single-notch rotors I–V. Verify this shortcut's error rate empirically for your specific target before relying on it.

**Non-clashing rule.** As an opportunistic speed-up when adjacent days' keys are already known (e.g., from breaking other messages of nearby dates), they note that **German operational practice never reused a rotor in the same physical position on two consecutive days** ("non-clashing rule," p. 15 of the PDF) — knowing one day's wheel order can eliminate up to half the 60 (or, by extension, 336) candidate wheel orders for the adjacent day. This is *not* available for an isolated single intercept with no neighboring known-key traffic (which appears to be your situation).

**Result: the CFYZR break, 72 letters, ciphertext-only.** This is the single most important data point in this survey. Full details, including the reproducible test vector, are in §2.5.

**Hardware and calibration point [primary].** Their reference machine was a single **Intel i7-3770 @ 3.4 GHz** (2013-era 4-core desktop). A sample decipherment of a 50-letter text took **~100 ns**, i.e., ~10 million candidate texts/second on that CPU. Exhausting the **full 60-wheel-order Army space with the fast "E-Stecker"/frequency-weighted methods** took on the order of **hours per wheel order per CPU core**; with all 4 cores/8 threads in parallel, **the full 60-wheel-order space was exhausted in ≈28 hours** for one of their example short messages (YYBRW, 46 letters). Full plugboard exhaustion (150 trillion arrangements) at each of the 27,418,560 locations would need ≈15 million seconds (**about half a year**) of raw compute — illustrating exactly why partial exhaustion + hillclimbing, not brute force of the plugboard, is the only tractable approach (this is explicit in the paper, §5).

### 2.5 Verified reproducible test vector: a genuine 72-letter break

**Use this to validate your implementation before attacking the real target.** The following is transcribed directly from the appendix of Ostwald & Weierud (2017); I independently counted the ciphertext letters (72, matching the paper's stated length) but have **not** run this key through an Enigma simulator myself to confirm it decrypts to the stated plaintext — treat that decryption step as the first thing your own implementation should do, not as already confirmed by me. Message No. 203 of HG Nord, sent **14 July 1941**, discriminant group **CFYZR** (the discriminant/Kenngruppe is *not* part of the ciphertext and is not counted in the 72-letter length):

```
Key:  B531  lwb  BER   BT CH DR EW FU GK JO LV MS PZ
      (reflector B, wheel order 5-3-1, ring setting l-w-b,
       start position B-E-R, ten stecker pairs as listed;
       unplugged/self-steckered letters: A I N Q X Y)

Ciphertext (72 letters, discriminant CFYZR excluded):
NFOSO IFKXN EMBCX CWMSC MORVY WSVHF BZJHN EMQFW
ZQOLU IZBFF BSNKS QXSHR DAMFR SESGJ JD

Plaintext (emended/translated): "An Roem Eins Berta x Quartiermeispcr
Panz x Gruppe x Ostrow Ostrow x Kasernengelzenme" — "To Roman One B
[Ib is the Chief of Supply] Quartermaster Tank Group Ostrov Ostrov
barracks area."
```

Per the paper, this exact 72-letter message was broken by their software using the **"I-Stecker"** partial-exhaustion method, in **under 5 days wall-clock time on 4 CPU cores working different wheel orders in parallel, at ≈28 core-hours per wheel order** — but with an important caveat: because the wheel order for the *adjacent* day (13 July) was already known from another broken message, the **non-clashing rule cut the search from the full 60 wheel orders down to 24** (§2.4). **[DERIVED]** At 28 core-hours/wheel-order and 4 cores, exhausting all 24 candidate wheel orders would take ≈(24×28)/4 = 168 hours (7 days); they report breaking it in under 5 days, meaning the correct key was found before the search was complete. **[DERIVED]** Without any adjacent-day information (the realistic case for a genuinely isolated 72-letter intercept), exhausting the full 60 wheel orders at the same rate would take ≈(60×28)/4 = 420 hours (**≈17.5 days**) on this 2013-era 4-core desktop.

Other genuine short HG Nord breaks documented with full keys/ciphertexts in the same paper's appendix (useful as additional regression tests, letter counts verified by me from the transcribed ciphertext): **AMERI** (32 letters, the shortest they broke), **PFCXY** (36 letters), **YYBRW** (46 letters, broken in <2 hours/wheel order single-core), **HODSN** (48 letters), **BOTKB** (69 letters, "nearly unbreakable" despite its length, due to an unlucky low-IC plaintext), **ABPQX** (76 letters, also nearly unbreakable for the same reason), and **FHPQX** (214 letters, the first genuine break, 17 March 2003). Note: the paper describes CFYZR as "a singular and still unbroken short message" *before* recounting how they themselves broke it in this same paper — that framing refers to its status prior to their 2017 work, not a currently-open problem. Treat CFYZR strictly as your **solved regression-test vector** (key, ciphertext, and plaintext all given above), not as a second target to attack.

### 2.6 A real naval ciphertext-only break: U-534, 128 letters

Michael Hörenberg's project (enigma.hoerenberg.com, founded 2012, working from Enigma material recovered from **U-534** in 1993) documents an actual **naval M4 ciphertext-only break**: a **128-letter** message, broken using his **TBreaker** software with **IC plus German bi- and trigram scoring**, described as finding the correct message key "very fast" (no more precise timing given on the page). Decrypted plaintext: *"VVVUUUVIRSOBENNULEINSXX MITUUUVIRSIBENNULZWOY VIRSIBENNULDREIYZWODREISECHSEINSYZWODREIDREIACHTEINSDREIOITNACHWZSTENPASSIRT"* — a U-boat position report ("From U-4701: with U-4702, 4703, 2361 and 2338, passed [point] 13 Red, [headed] west").

This is the closest published analogue I found to your actual problem (naval, ciphertext-only, short), but at 128 letters it is still ~1.8× longer than your 72-letter target, and I found **no published naval M4 ciphertext-only break shorter than this** in the sources searched. **[NOT ESTABLISHED IN SOURCES FOUND]:** a specific, sourced "minimum practical length" figure for ciphertext-only M4 attacks. Do not treat the Army figures in §2.4–2.5 as directly transferable to M4 without accounting for the keyspace multiplier in §4.

### 2.7 On Lasry/Kopal/Wacker and short-message claims — correcting scope

Be careful with this attribution, which is easy to get wrong: **George Lasry, Nils Kopal, and Arno Wacker's "Cryptanalysis of Enigma double indicators with hill climbing" (Cryptologia 43(4):267–292, 2019)** is about recovering keys from the **Army/Luftwaffe *double-indicator* procedures used before September 1938 and from September 1938–May 1940** — it does **not** apply to naval Enigma's Kenngruppenbuch indicator system (§1.5), which has no indicator-doubling to exploit. A separate, frequently-conflated result — **"as short as 500 letters, compared to 750–1,250 letters with prior methods," using nested hill-climbing and simulated annealing** — is from Lasry's broader work on **the Hagelin M-209**, a different cipher machine entirely, **not Enigma**. I could not find a Lasry-authored Enigma-specific "minimum ciphertext-only length" figure in the sources searched; do not cite the 500/750–1250 figures as Enigma numbers.

Lasry's PhD thesis ("A Methodology for the Cryptanalysis of Classical Ciphers with Search Metaheuristics," Kassel, 2017/2018) likely contains additional Enigma-relevant material (nested hillclimbing, simulated annealing generalized across several classical ciphers) but was not read in depth for this survey; treat as a lead for follow-up, not a source of specific numbers here.

### 2.8 CrypTool 2's Enigma Analyzer

CrypTool 2 (open-source, Windows/.NET, actively maintained by the Kassel group) ships an "Enigma Analyzer" component offering **six different computerized attacks**, described in Kopal et al., "New Ciphers and Cryptanalysis Components in CrypTool 2" (HistoCrypt 2022). It demonstrably performs a **hillclimbing attack on a short (≈200-letter) Enigma message** as a built-in demo. This is a usable off-the-shelf tool for cross-checking your own implementation, though as a GUI/workflow tool it is less suited to unattended long-running search than a purpose-built CLI.

## 3. Known-plaintext / crib-assisted attacks on short messages

If you have *any* crib — even a low-confidence, partial one — the problem changes character entirely: instead of searching for statistical distinguishability in noise, you're testing an algebraic hypothesis (does this rotor-order/position/stecker-partial produce this specific plaintext fragment at this specific offset?), which is both cheaper to check per-candidate and dramatically more decisive when it succeeds. This is why Bletchley Park's whole apparatus (menus, loops, Bombe) was built around cribs rather than statistics, and it's why the Hörenberg project — attacking messages of comparable length to yours — leans on cribs wherever possible rather than pure ciphertext-only search.

### 3.1 Message-format cribs

Naval and U-boat message traffic had strong, well-documented structural regularities that function as cribs even without knowing the substantive content:

- **Procedural markers, in clear vs. possibly in-body.** The priority marker **"Kr" (Kriegsmeldung, "combat report")** visible in the message-sheet preamble in Ostwald & Weierud's Figure 7 is confirmed **transmitted in clear, outside the enciphered body**, for that Army message — it's metadata, not a crib against that ciphertext. Whether an urgent-message marker like **"KR KR"** ever recurs *inside* the enciphered body of naval traffic (e.g., as an in-text procedure sign, the way "UUU" does — see below) is **[NOT ESTABLISHED IN SOURCES FOUND]**; don't assume it's a usable in-body crib without separately confirming naval message-format conventions for your specific network/period.
- **The spelling alphabet.** The Wehrmacht used a fixed set of code words for individual letters when spelling things out (e.g., **"Siegfried"** for S, **"Toni"** for T) — Ostwald & Weierud explicitly note message FHPQX is a "typical example... that possibly could have been broken at BP with the aid of a crib," citing **"SiegfriedSiegfried"** or **"SiegfriedSiegfriedToni"** as the plausible crib, and reproduce a Turing-Welchman-Bombe simulation that recovers the correct ten steckers from this crib alone "within seconds."
- **Weather report abbreviations.** "**WEUB**" and numeric-coded weather groups appear as recurring, guessable openers in weather-related traffic (Wikipedia "Cryptanalysis of the Enigma," cited example: *WEUBYYNULLSEQSNULLNULL* ≈ "weather survey 0600").
- **Naval position-report conventions, confirmed from a real decrypt.** The U-534 128-letter break (§2.6) decrypts to `VVVUUUVIRSOBENNULEINSXX MITUUUVIRSIBENNULZWOY VIRSIBENNULDREIYZWODREISECHSEINSYZWODREIDREIACHTEINSDREIOITNACHWZSTENPASSIRT` — this is **not** a codebook message; it's plain military German with digits spelled out phonetically (VIR/VIER=4, SIBEN=7, NUL=0, EINS=1, ZWO=2, DREI=3, SECHS=6, ACHT=8), using **"UUU"** before U-boat numbers as a procedure sign, **"Y"** as a separator between number groups, **"XX"** as a break/punctuation marker, and closing with "…nach Westen passiert" (≈"…passed heading west"). These are strong, sourced, reusable cribs/corpus features for naval traffic generally: **train your n-gram scorer on naval-style text with spelled-out digits and these procedure signs**, not on generic German prose, and consider testing candidate decryptions specifically for spelled-digit patterns (EINS, ZWO, DREI, VIER, FUNF, SECHS, SIBEN, ACHT, NEUN, NUL) as a targeted crib/scoring signal independent of general language statistics.

### 3.2 Kurzsignale — a caveat, but probably not the right model for 72 letters

If your target were a genuine **Kurzsignal** (short signal — a compressed position/sighting report using the **Kurzsignalheft** codebook) or **Wetterkurzschlüssel** (short weather cipher) message, standard ciphertext-only n-gram scoring would need adjustment, because those messages encode **fixed codebook groups** rather than free German prose. However, **this is probably not what a 72-letter message is**: per Wikipedia's "Kurzsignale" article, the short signal system was built so a full report compressed to **about 25 characters**, transmittable in **about 20 seconds**, specifically to minimize exposure to Allied direction-finding — **72 letters is about three times that length**, which argues *against* your target being a Kurzsignal proper, not for it. The confirmed real naval decrypt available (U-534, §2.6, quoted above) is, despite being a compressed position report in content, **plain abbreviated German, not codebook groups** — which is exactly why it broke with ordinary IC/bigram/trigram scoring in the first place. Best working assumption: your 72-letter target is most likely plain (if terse, abbreviated, jargon-heavy) naval German of the kind §2.4's HG Nord corpus and the U-534 decrypt both exemplify, and standard n-gram scoring trained on that style of text should apply — but a true codebook-group message can't be ruled out from length alone, so if your best-scoring candidates all look like meaningless letter noise even after exhausting the search space, revisit this assumption before concluding the attack failed.

### 3.3 How the Hörenberg project actually operates

Based on the project's own methodology pages: the standard workflow is (1) find the **daily key** (Tagesschlüssel) using a **Turing-Bombe simulator** ("M4 WinEnigma Turingbombe" software) against a crib on one message of the day, which is the computationally expensive step; (2) once the daily key is known, use the much faster **TBreaker** hillclimbing tool to determine each individual message's **start position** and any remaining plugs; (3) decrypt all other messages sharing that daily key trivially. The project explicitly states that **"finding cribs was not at all straightforward; it required considerable familiarity with German military jargon"** — i.e., crib-hunting for naval traffic is a domain-knowledge-intensive, manual step, not something that drops out of generic tooling. For a genuinely isolated single 72-letter message with no sister traffic sharing a key, step (3)'s advantage (free decrypts of everything else that day) is unavailable, and you're relying on step (1)/(2) against this one message alone — closer in difficulty to the U-534 128-letter ciphertext-only case (§2.6) than to the "break one, get many free" case that naval cryptanalysis (historical and modern) usually relies on.

## 4. The real complexity: keyspace, and what collapses it

### 4.1 Rotor orders

- **Army/Luftwaffe (5 rotors, I–V):** P(5,3) = 5×4×3 = **60** wheel orders.
- **Naval M3 (8 rotors, I–VIII; VI/VII/VIII have two notches instead of one):** P(8,3) = 8×7×6 = **336** wheel orders. **[DERIVED]** This is **336/60 = 5.6×** the Army search just from wheel-order count.
- **Naval M4 (4 rotors: 3 rotating from the same 8, plus a non-rotating 4th "Greek" wheel Beta or Gamma):** the 3 rotating wheels still give 336 orders; separately, the 4th wheel is **not accounted for by 8×7×6×2 = 672** the way the task brief's example formula suggests. Because the 4th wheel never steps, its ring setting and start position are degenerate with each other (only their *offset* matters), and it pairs with one of two dedicated thin reflectors (UKW B-thin or C-thin). The correct multiplier is: **2 (Beta/Gamma) × 2 (B-thin/C-thin) × 26 (net offset) = 104** combinations to search **in addition to** the 336 three-wheel orders — giving **336 × 104 = 34,944** total "extended wheel order" combinations for M4, not 672. **[DERIVED, not sourced]** — this differs from the task brief's illustrative 672 figure; I'm flagging the correction rather than silently using the brief's number. I did not find a source asserting that Beta is mechanically restricted to pairing only with B-thin (or Gamma only with C-thin) — treat any such restriction as unverified; if true it would let you drop the ×2 reflector factor, so it's worth confirming empirically against a reference Enigma simulator (e.g., set Beta at position A, ring A, with UKW B-thin, and check it reproduces plain UKW-B M3 behavior exactly — this is a good simulator correctness unit test regardless).

### 4.2 Positions, ring settings, and the "collapse" tricks

- Naively: 26³ = 17,576 start positions × 26² = 676 ring settings (middle+right; left-wheel ring is provably irrelevant to the cipher, independent of message length, since it only shifts an already-arbitrary absolute rotation) = 11,881,376 combinations per wheel order.
- **Short-message collapse (Ostwald & Weierud, Army-validated):** for messages short enough that the **left-hand wheel is unlikely to step at all**, the middle-wheel ring setting becomes irrelevant too (no turnover event depends on it), leaving only the right-hand wheel's 26 ring settings — collapsing to **26³ × 26 = 26⁴ = 456,976** "locations" per wheel order. This is the trick that makes the Army 72-letter break in §2.5 computationally tractable at all.
- **Naval caveat, my own derivation [DERIVED, verify empirically]:** this collapse assumes a low left-wheel-turnover probability, which Ostwald & Weierud estimate at ~10% for a ~72-letter Army message with single-notch wheels I–V. For naval traffic, three of the eight available rotors (VI, VII, VIII) have **two notches** instead of one. The probability that the left-hand wheel steps at least once, given a message of length L, a middle-wheel notch count n, and k ≈ (L / 26) × (right-hand-wheel notch count) middle-wheel steps, is approximately **P ≈ k·n / 26**. Working this out for L=72: with a **single-notch right wheel** (k≈72/26≈2.77) and **single-notch middle wheel** (n=1): P≈2.77/26≈10.6% (matches the paper's ~10% figure, confirming the model). With **both right and middle wheels double-notch** (a wheel order using two of VI/VII/VIII in those two positions): k≈2.77×2≈5.54, n=2, giving **P≈5.54×2/26≈43%**. With **only one of the two positions double-notch**: P≈2.77×2/26≈21% either way. Averaged over all naval wheel-order/position assignments, expect roughly **20–40% of naval wheel orders to have a non-trivial chance of a left-wheel turnover within a 72-letter message** — meaning the "ignore the middle-wheel ring entirely" shortcut is unsafe for a meaningful fraction of the search space. **[DERIVED] The actual cost of handling this correctly is much smaller than a full 676-value middle-ring search, though, once you notice what the middle-wheel ring setting actually controls:** you are already exhausting the middle wheel's *position* (it's part of the 26³ position search); the ring setting only shifts *at which of the middle wheel's own step-events, if any, its notch aligns with the pawl and causes the left wheel to step*. Over a 72-letter message the middle wheel steps only **k ≈ 3–6 times** (k≈2.77 for a single-notch right wheel, k≈5.5 for a double-notch one, per the calculation above) — so the left-wheel turnover can only occur at one of those **k step-events, or not at all: k+1 distinguishable cases, not 26.** That means the correct extra cost of handling the naval double-notch case properly is on the order of **×4–7 (k+1)** on top of the already-searched position/right-ring space, not ×26 and nowhere near the ×676 of a naive full ring search. **Recommendation: implement the real double-step stepping mechanism in your simulator (do not skip it — it's cheap to implement correctly and expensive to get wrong), and represent the middle-wheel ring search as "which of the k middle-wheel step-events (if any) triggers the left-wheel turnover" (k+1 cases) rather than as a raw 26-value ring sweep.** Separately, since rotors VI/VII/VIII have their two notches **13 positions apart** (at letters Z and M — **[DERIVED from rotor wiring data, verify against your simulator]**), a double-notch *right-hand* wheel likely has only **13 distinct stepping-timing phases** rather than 26, which would additionally roughly halve the right-ring search specifically for wheel orders using a double-notch wheel in the right-hand position — verify this symmetry empirically before relying on it, it is not asserted in any source found.

### 4.3 The plugboard

10 stecker cables among 26 letters (leaving 6 unplugged) has **150,738,274,937,250** (≈151 trillion) possible arrangements (Ostwald & Weierud 2017, verified formula 26!/((26−2k)!·k!·2^k) for k=10). This dwarfs every other factor combined and **cannot be brute-forced**; every method surveyed here (Gillogly onward) substitutes **hillclimbing, seeded by partial exhaustion of the first few plugs**, for exhaustive plugboard search (§2.2, §2.4).

### 4.4 What this means for search strategy

The practical, load-bearing search loop (validated by every source above) is:

```
for wheel_order in candidates:          # 60 (army) / 336 (naval M3) / 34,944 (naval M4)
    for position in 26^3:                # or fold into the next loop, see below
        for right_ring in 26:             # the "ignore-middle-ring" shortcut, naval-caveated
            partial_exhaust_first_plugs() # solo/E-Stecker/R-Stecker/I-Stecker, §2.4
            hillclimb_remaining_plugs(scoring = trigram, fallback bigram/hexagram)
            score_candidate()
    keep best-scoring candidate(s) per wheel_order for manual/wordlist review
```

Note the outer wheel-order loop is what's parallelized across cores/GPU threads in every real implementation surveyed (Ostwald & Weierud's 4-cores-4-wheel-orders scheme; the M4 Project's per-client work units, §5).

## 5. Can we actually run this locally? GPU/distributed tools and realistic timing

### 5.1 Existing tools you could run today

- **enigma-cuda** (github.com/VE3NEA/enigma-cuda) — GPU-accelerated (CUDA), command-line-compatible with Stefan Krah's enigma-suite (the same codebase used by Enigma@Home), explicitly implements the Ostwald & Weierud partial-exhaustion method plus hillclimbing. Recommends an Nvidia GTX 1070 or better (i.e., modest, consumer-grade hardware even by 2017 standards — a genuinely modern GPU should do substantially better). No published benchmark numbers were found in its README, but it is a direct, ready-to-run implementation of exactly the method in §2.4, for M3 *and* M4 ("Heer, M3, or M4 Enigma machines" per its own description).
- **bruteforce-enigma** (github.com/the-lambda-way/bruteforce-enigma) — CPU (C++20), rotor-order/position/ring brute force (no plugboard search built in — you'd need to add hillclimbing yourself). Published benchmark on a Core i7 @ 2.5GHz, 4 threads: **252.3 million characters/second, 1.48 million full decryptions/second**, exhausting rotor+ring+position for a **170-character** message (all rotor/ring/position combinations, no plugboard) in **33 minutes**; a 56-character cipher (with its "smart decipher" heuristic mode) solved in **1.6 seconds**. Useful as a lower bound on how fast raw rotor/position exhaustion can go on ordinary CPU hardware today.
- **CrypTool 2** — GUI, six built-in Enigma attacks including hillclimbing, good for cross-validating your own implementation's output rather than for large unattended runs (§2.8).
- **TBreaker / M4 WinEnigma Turingbombe** (Hörenberg project tools) — Windows-native, used for real naval M4 breaks including the 128-letter U-534 case (§2.6, §3.3); worth obtaining as a reference/cross-check implementation even if you build your own primary pipeline.
- **Stefan Krah's enigma-suite / Enigma@Home** — the original distributed-computing codebase (2006 M4 Project) that both enigma-cuda and (per its own description) TBreaker-style tools descend from or are compatible with.

### 5.2 Distributed-computing precedent: the M4 Project (2006, third message 2013)

Stefan Krah's **M4 Message Breaking Project** (January 2006) targeted three genuine, previously-Bletchley-unbroken naval M4 messages (identified by Ralph Erskine), using volunteer BOINC-style distributed computing (brute-force wheel-order/position search + hillclimbing). Concrete figures from the project's own site (bytereef.org):
- Work units: **26⁴ keys per unit**, against **180-letter** ciphertexts, **7,434 total work units** for the full search space.
- Reference client speed: **~80 minutes per work unit on a 1.2 GHz Celeron** (a very slow, single-threaded reference machine even for 2006).
- Project's own estimate: **"100 participants running the software 24/7 could walk through the search space in 4 days."**
- **First message broken 20 February 2006** (weeks after launch, thousands of volunteers); **second message broken 7 March 2006**; the **third message was dramatically harder and was not broken until 14 January 2013**, by **Dan Girard** (with assistance from Michael Hörenberg), after what Girard's own account describes as roughly ten years of "innumerable runs of my Bombe-simulator program" — a concrete, sourced illustration that **"naval M4, ciphertext-only" is not uniformly tractable even at ~180 letters**, and that some individual messages resist the standard pipeline far longer than others. **I could not find a stated reason for the ten-year gap** (Hörenberg's write-up promises but does not include a methodology explanation in the page fetched) — don't assume it was necessarily "unlucky statistics" analogous to §2.4's BOTKB/ABPQX cases; it may equally have involved a crib, a wheel-order deduction, or another shortcut not documented in the source found. Treat the ten-year gap as evidence that naval M4 ciphertext-only difficulty is highly message-dependent and can be severe, without over-interpreting the specific cause.

This is a useful, sobering calibration: 180-letter naval M4 messages, attacked by thousands of volunteer CPUs, took **weeks (two of three) to years (one of three)**. Your 72-letter target is shorter — meaning the wheel-order/position/ring stage should be roughly comparably fast (it's dominated by search-space size, not message length, except that scoring reliability *degrades* at 72 letters per §2.4) — but the crucial extra difficulty is that at 72 letters the **statistical scoring stage itself becomes unreliable** in a way it mostly isn't yet at 180 letters, per §2.4's finding that the collapse happens under ~80 letters.

### 5.3 Realistic timing estimate for a 72-letter naval message on one modern Linux machine — [DERIVED, explicit chain shown]

Anchoring on Ostwald & Weierud's one directly-comparable calibration point — **their 72-letter CFYZR break: I-Stecker method, ≈28 core-hours per wheel order, on a 2013 Intel i7-3770** (§2.5) — and showing the multiplier chain explicitly rather than asserting a bottom-line number as if it were sourced:

| Case | Wheel-order-equivalents searched | Core-hours (at 28 h/order) | Wall-clock, 4 cores, 2013 CPU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Army (calibration case) | 60 | 1,680 | **≈17.5 days** |
| Naval M3 | 336 (**×5.6** vs Army) | 9,408 | **≈98 days (~3.3 months)** |
| Naval M4 | 336 × 104 = 34,944 (**×580** vs Army) | 978,432 | **≈10,192 days (~28 years)** |

These are **derived extrapolations from a single calibration point, not literature values** — treat them as order-of-magnitude planning numbers, not predictions. Two things move them substantially in the feasible direction:

1. **Hardware, 2013→2026.** A modern 16–64-core desktop/workstation CPU plus a modern GPU (enigma-cuda-class tooling) plausibly gives a combined throughput improvement of **one to two orders of magnitude** over a single 2013 4-core desktop for this kind of embarrassingly-parallel, per-candidate-cheap search (more cores, higher IPC/clock, and GPU offload of the innermost decrypt-and-score loop, which is exactly the kind of tight numeric loop GPUs excel at). A conservative **~100–300×** speedup is plausible without exotic infrastructure — a single good workstation with one or two modern GPUs, not a cluster.
2. **Cheaper partial-exhaustion variants.** I-Stecker (136 first-plug cases) is on the slow end of Ostwald & Weierud's own menu; **E-Stecker (26 cases) is reported as >10× faster than "solo" (325 cases)**, and by extension meaningfully faster than I-Stecker too, at some cost in reliability (fewer of the "most informative" letters pre-exhausted). A faster-but-noisier first pass, with I-Stecker or trio/quartet as a fallback only where E-Stecker's candidates look promising, is a reasonable engineering trade.

Combining a **~150× hardware speedup** with **no change in method** brings naval M3 down to **≈16 hours** and naval M4 down to **≈68 days** — i.e., **naval M3 at 72 letters looks tractable on a single modern Linux machine within a day or so of compute**, while **naval M4 at 72 letters, pure brute-force-plus-hillclimb with no side information, is a multi-week-to-multi-month undertaking on a single machine even with generous modern-hardware assumptions**. Correctly handling §4.2's naval double-notch turnover issue (the k+1-case middle-wheel treatment, roughly ×4–7, rather than the far cheaper but unsafe "ignore it" shortcut) adds a modest, tolerable overhead on this scale — it does **not** push M4 into the years range on its own; the earlier draft of this estimate overstated that cost (a full 676-value middle-ring sweep would have, but that's not what's actually required — see §4.2's correction). The dominant driver of the M3→M4 gap is the ×104 fourth-wheel/reflector factor (§4.1), not the ring-setting handling.

**Any side information collapses this dramatically**, the same way it did historically (§1.5–1.6) and in Ostwald & Weierud's own non-clashing-rule trick (§2.5): a crib of any kind (§3), a known or guessable network/date that narrows the rotor set actually in use, or (if you have other intercepts from the same key period) the non-clashing rule or shared-daily-key decryption. **Pure ciphertext-only naval M4 with zero side information at 72 letters is the hard case; treat every piece of available context about the message (date, originating network, U-boat identity if known, message format) as worth spending real effort to recover before falling back to brute-force-plus-hillclimb.**

## 6. Summary and candid odds assessment

**What's well-established:**
- A 72-letter, **Army** (3-rotor, 60-wheel-order), steckered Wehrmacht Enigma message has been broken **ciphertext-only, reproducibly, with the full key and plaintext published** (CFYZR, §2.5) — build and validate your pipeline against this first.
- A 128-letter, **naval M4** message has been broken **ciphertext-only** (U-534, §2.6), using essentially the same IC→n-gram→hillclimbing pipeline, on unspecified but apparently modest hardware, "very fast."
- The theoretical floor (unicity distance) for this class of cipher is **~20–24 letters** for the Army case (§2.4) — below that, no amount of compute can succeed in principle, because the ciphertext doesn't contain enough information to uniquely determine the key. **[DERIVED]** Naval M4's larger keyspace (§4.1, roughly ×580 vs. the Army case, ≈9–10 more bits) pushes the unicity distance up only slightly, to roughly **≈26 letters** (unicity distance scales with log of keyspace, which grows far more slowly than the keyspace itself). **72 letters is comfortably above this floor for every variant (M3 or M4)** — length alone does not rule out success; the practical obstacle is scoring reliability and search cost (§2.4, §5.3), not information-theoretic impossibility.
- The dominant real failure mode at short lengths is **not raw compute** but **scoring unreliability**: the first few plugboard plugs cannot be found by any known statistical criterion under ~80 letters (§2.4), which is why partial exhaustion (E-/R-/I-Stecker) rather than pure hillclimbing is essential, and why individual messages with unlucky low-IC plaintext (BOTKB, ABPQX in §2.4/§2.5) can remain "nearly unbreakable" by these methods even when substantially longer than 72 letters.

**Odds, if your target is Army/Heer-style 3-rotor Enigma (60 wheel orders):** Good. This is a solved problem at this exact length, with a published, reproducible method and a demonstrated real example at exactly 72 letters. Ciphertext-only alone should work, on the order of days to a couple of weeks on modern hardware, *provided* the individual message's underlying plaintext isn't one of the unlucky low-IC cases that even the paper's authors couldn't crack (this is genuinely message-dependent and can't be predicted in advance — you find out by trying).

**Odds, if your target is naval M3 (336 wheel orders, no 4th rotor):** Reasonably good but meaningfully harder — roughly 5–6× the search space of the solved Army case, further complicated by the double-notch rotor turnover issue (§4.2) that the published short-message methodology doesn't address (it was developed and validated on Army traffic). Very plausibly tractable on a single modern machine (GPU-assisted) within hours to a few days, *if* the message plaintext isn't statistically unlucky and isn't a Kurzsignal/codebook message (§3.2) that would defeat standard n-gram scoring regardless of compute.

**Odds, if your target is naval M4 (4 rotors, 336×104 extended wheel orders):** The honest, load-bearing statement is: **pure ciphertext-only at 72 letters with zero side information and no crib is the genuinely hard case surveyed here, and I found no published example of it being done at this length.** The closest published naval M4 ciphertext-only success is 128 letters (§2.6); the closest published *fully worked, reproducible* naval M4 ciphertext-only method at a comparable short length simply wasn't found in the literature searched — this gap (marked §2.6, §5.3) is itself informative: it suggests either that no one has published a systematic short-naval-message study analogous to Ostwald & Weierud's Army study, or that it's harder than the Army case in a way that's discouraged publication of a clean success, or both. **Derived timing estimates (§5.3) span from ~16 hours to ~28 years depending on hardware assumptions, method choices, and how the double-notch turnover issue is handled** — a wide enough range that "will it work" depends heavily on implementation quality, not just raw compute availability. **Any crib, message-format clue, or contextual information (date, network, likely codebook-message structure) should be pursued hard before committing to a long unattended brute-force run**, because — as at Bletchley Park in 1941–42 — the naval case was never solved by brute force alone, historically or in the modern ciphertext-only literature; it was solved by combining computation with *exploiting structure* (indicator systems then, message-format/codebook priors now).

**Recommended sequencing for implementation:** (1) build a correct Enigma simulator, including the double-step anomaly, and unit-test it against the CFYZR vector in §2.5 and, ideally, a hand-verified M4 configuration; (2) implement the IC-then-partial-exhaustion-then-trigram-hillclimb pipeline of §2.4/§4.4 and validate it reproduces CFYZR's key and plaintext from ciphertext alone; (3) run it against the target assuming Army/M3 first (cheap, and rules out the possibility the message isn't actually 4-rotor naval traffic at all — this is a real possibility worth checking rather than assuming); (4) if unsuccessful, extend to the full naval M4 search per §4.1/§5.3, budgeting for multi-day-to-multi-week runs and parallelizing across all available cores/GPU; (5) throughout, keep a parallel track hunting for any crib or contextual metadata (§3) — this is the single highest-leverage thing that could turn a multi-week brute-force gamble into a near-certain, fast, crib-anchored break.
